Three ways cc-control could feel

Each direction fixes the same audit findings (docs/UX-AUDIT.md): a visible New task button, projects instead of paths, plain-language status and approvals, a readable transcript, quiet key hints and a light theme. They differ in what the app is organised around. Every existing feature and every keyboard shortcut survives in all three; the table at the end shows where each one lives.

A · InboxB · ProjectsC · FocusFeature map

Direction A

Inbox organised by what needs you

  • Home is one calm list in three sections: Needs you, Working, Done, then Earlier. Projects live in a sidebar as filters.
  • A task reads like a Messages thread. Claude's actions fold into a "steps" summary with real file changes you can open.
  • Approvals rise as a sheet: what, where, how risky, three clear buttons.
  • Shortcuts are a row of named chips above the message box.

Strong at

  • The first screen answers "what needs me?" without any learning.
  • Most familiar shape (Mail, Reminders, Messages), so the least to explain in a demo.
  • Scales from 1 task to 20 without changing layout.

Watch out for

  • Projects are secondary (a sidebar filter), so "what have I done in Payments API?" takes a click.
  • The shortcut chips show about six; the rest are one click away in "More…".

Direction B

Projects organised by where the work is

  • Home is a set of project cards, like rooms in the Home app. Each card shows its tasks and their state. A banner on top says what needs you.
  • A task is a split view: the conversation on the left, a live Activity panel on the right (steps, files changed, background work).
  • Approvals appear at the top of the Activity panel, and in the banner on every screen.
  • Shortcuts keep a 3×3 grid, now as labelled tiles with icons; the number is a small corner badge.

Strong at

  • Matches how people think about their work ("my web app"), and makes projects feel like named, iconed things.
  • The Activity panel gives the most transparency: every step, every changed file, background work.
  • Keeps the 3×3 shortcut grid for numpad users, with readable labels.

Watch out for

  • With many projects, "what needs me" relies on the banner rather than the layout.
  • The split view is busier; on a phone the Activity panel becomes a tab.

Direction C

Focus organised around one question

  • Home is a single large box, "What should Claude do?", with a project picker built into it. Tasks sit quietly underneath.
  • A task reads like a document: one centred column, a floating status capsule at the top with Stop.
  • An approval replaces the message box until you answer, so there is only ever one thing to do.
  • Shortcuts appear as suggestions under the box, and in full with /.

Strong at

  • The calmest and most "Apple" of the three; one obvious action on every screen.
  • The docked approval can't be missed or answered by accident, and typing a new instruction instead is natural.
  • Best for a live demo: open, type, watch.

Watch out for

  • With several tasks running, the home list gets short shrift under the big box.
  • Least visible power: shortcuts, background work and history are one step further away.

What all three share

  • Words: "Waiting for your OK", "Working on it", "Done", "Stopped". Tools become verbs ("Reading index.html", "Running a check"). One tested module owns every label, plus a safe / careful / risky rating for approvals.
  • Keys: every current shortcut still works, including numpad, Alt+N, Y/A/N (now without the Tab step), Esc to stop, Ctrl+K and ?. Hints show on hover and focus, and a "Show shortcuts" setting brings back the always-on hint bar.
  • Look: light and dark following the system, 15px body text, AA contrast, visible focus rings, 40px+ targets, labelled icon buttons.
  • Projects: named after their folder, with a colour and initial; rename and recolour is optional.

Recommendation

A · Inbox, borrowing two pieces: B's "files changed" list inside the steps card, and C's rule that an approval replaces the message box on phones. A answers "what needs me?" by its very layout, which is the owner's first complaint about lists, and it is the easiest to demo to people who have never seen Claude Code. B is the better fit if most use is one project at a time; C if the demo is one task, start to finish.
